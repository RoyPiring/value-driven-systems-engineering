## The Vision: Building a Quality-Controlled Design Studio

### Why this system exists

I am building this AI Product Design Studio to establish a professional, quality-controlled design pipeline where every artifact must achieve a minimum 7/10 approval threshold before reaching the client. The objective is not only generating design assets faster but creating a governed delivery system that combines intake, research, generation, review, and learning into one repeatable workflow.

By integrating Claude Desktop, Paper, Obsidian, and Firecrawl, the studio automates client brief ingestion, competitive research, design generation, and evaluation while simultaneously creating a Continuous Intelligence layer. Every approval, rejection, and revision becomes reusable operational knowledge that informs future work.

This shifts the workflow from isolated design projects into a scalable system capable of increasing first-pass approvals, reducing rework cycles, and tightening delivery efficiency over time.

### How parallel windows multiply output

Parallel execution works because each Claude session maintains independent context while sharing the same operational infrastructure.

The Client pipeline focuses on delivery work and artifact generation.

The Approval pipeline manages governance, scoring, and review workflows.

Although contexts remain isolated, all sessions share access to the same Studio vault and MCP services. Outputs generated in one workflow immediately become accessible to the others.

This converts sequential design activity into parallel throughput while preserving quality boundaries.

## Constructing the Vault, 25 Agents, and Approval Scorecard

### Step overview

This phase established the operational intelligence layer and governance foundation of the design studio.

Using the filesystem MCP, I created a structured PARA-based Studio vault to organize active projects, resources, references, archives, and intelligence artifacts. The vault became the central repository for client briefs, design outputs, scorecards, proposal assets, Canon references, and Continuous Intelligence logs.

On top of that foundation, I generated 25 specialized agents covering research, branding, design production, proposal creation, governance, and intelligence capture. Each agent was embedded with role-specific design doctrine and approval logic so outputs followed measurable standards instead of subjective preference.

The governance layer introduced Council Lead, Standards Gate, Intelligence Agent, and the Artifact Approval Protocol, creating a controlled production workflow where quality validation occurs throughout execution rather than only at delivery.

![Image](https://learn.nextwork.org/refreshed_maroon_timid_jujube/uploads/b36e1450-1397-4aa1-87c5-afbc04401299_egv6tb6p)

### How the Approval Criteria gates quality

The Approval Criteria system replaced generic reviews with agent-specific evaluation frameworks.

Each agent received dedicated 1–10 scoring dimensions with explicit definitions describing acceptable execution within that role. Logo agents focused on composition, scalability, originality, and brand fit. Research agents evaluated market coverage, insight quality, and data freshness. Brief agents validated scope clarity, audience alignment, and delivery readiness.

Every artifact entered the Artifact Approval Protocol before progressing downstream. Any dimension scoring below seven triggered rejection and routed the work back for refinement.

Escalation occurred after three unsuccessful cycles, ensuring governance remained embedded directly inside execution.

## Structuring 3 Austin Client Briefs with Scoring

### Step overview

In this phase, I ingested the client briefs for Casa Verde Taqueria, Graceful Home Care, and Eastside Capital into the design pipeline to validate project readiness before production started.

The objective was treating intake as a quality gate instead of an administrative activity. The Artifact Approval System evaluated each brief to identify scope gaps, timeline risks, stakeholder complexity, and hidden delivery dependencies before design work began.

Council Lead then executed routing classification to organize delivery paths and validate readiness against studio standards. This established a measurable baseline before artifact generation and reduced downstream rework risk.

![Image](https://learn.nextwork.org/refreshed_maroon_timid_jujube/uploads/b36e1450-1397-4aa1-87c5-afbc04401299_flnngko1)

### What the Approval System caught before production started

The review identified a scope, budget, and timeline mismatch. Requested deliverables aligned closer to a Premium-tier engagement ($4,500 / 3 weeks) while the planned schedule reflected a Recommended-tier timeline (2 weeks) and the budget sat between both ranges.

The process also surfaced hidden delivery dependencies. Regulatory review activities and possible external research requirements introduced more than 48 hours of additional effort that had not been operationally accounted for.

A third finding involved stakeholder complexity. Instead of a single reviewer, the effective audience expanded to approximately 8–12 participants, increasing approval expectations and creating additional delivery pressure.

This early validation prevented execution from starting with unresolved constraints and tightened planning accuracy before production work began.

## Running the Council and Approving Every Output

### Step overview

In this phase, I initiated competitive research for Casa Verde Taqueria using Firecrawl and evaluated the results against the studio intelligence dimensions: Market Coverage, Insight Quality, and Data Freshness.

After research validation, I executed the five Brand agents while enforcing the studio-wide 7/10 approval threshold across every artifact. Standards Gate then consolidated the outputs into the Council scorecard to establish the aggregate quality baseline for the review cycle.

The objective was ensuring artifacts entered visual production only after passing governance and structured evaluation instead of relying on subjective review.

![Image](https://learn.nextwork.org/refreshed_maroon_timid_jujube/uploads/b36e1450-1397-4aa1-87c5-afbc04401299_ljy6ldb5)

### Hardest scoring axis and what fixed it

The most difficult dimension was A2 Originality, becoming the only first-pass failure with a score of 6/10.

The original “Hand-pressed” concept differentiated well within the local Austin competitor set but showed overlap with broader artisan branding patterns commonly used across food, craft, and market ecosystems.

The correction introduced the “Marker” concept, shifting identity toward chalk-mark references tied to truck heritage and operational authenticity.

This raised Originality from 5 to 8 and increased the A2 composite score from 7.3 to 8.0, moving the artifact into approved status.

The finding was important because the issue emerged from broader-category pattern analysis rather than direct competitor comparison, creating a reusable Canon insight for future evaluations.

## Designing in Paper, Rating Visually, and Delivering the Proposal

### Step overview

In this phase, I used the E1 Paper Integration agent to transform approved Council outputs into visual concepts directly inside Paper.

The objective was ensuring every concept passed the studio quality threshold before entering proposal workflows. The process combined design production, structured scoring, and proposal preparation into a single governed delivery path.

Each concept was evaluated against the studio review framework and required a minimum 7/10 score across all dimensions before approval. This ensured visual completion alone was not enough to move artifacts downstream.

Approved concepts then transitioned into proposal packaging and delivery preparation.

### Iteration that raised the score

The largest refinement cycle occurred on Concept C – Salsa Mark, where Scalability scored 6/10, becoming the only failing dimension.

Feedback focused on design principle rather than visual preference: “Identity should survive through proportion rather than fragile detail.”

The redesign removed the independent splash-drop element and integrated it into the primary silhouette so asymmetry itself became the recognition signal.

This raised Scalability from 6 to 8 and lifted the composite score from 7.0 to 7.33 while preserving typography, color balance, painterly style, and negative-space relationships.

Status changed from REJECT → APPROVED.

### Final proposal scores across 4 axes

The proposal passed all four evaluation dimensions during the first review cycle:

Value Communication: 9
Anchoring Effectiveness: 8
Urgency: 9
Professionalism: 9

The final composite reached 8.75, resulting in first-pass approval without revisions.

Anchoring received the only eight because the Premium delivery timeline conflicted with the Day-22 signage schedule. Explicitly documenting this constraint slightly reduced anchor strength but raised transparency and professionalism.

Reviewer validation scored 8.5, remaining within tolerance and avoiding escalation.

Final decision status: SEND.

## Secret Mission: All 3 Clients Scored 8+, $9,000 in Proposals

![Image](https://learn.nextwork.org/refreshed_maroon_timid_jujube/uploads/b36e1450-1397-4aa1-87c5-afbc04401299_hv76l6vs)

### Strongest and weakest axes across all clients

Across the three simulated engagements, the strongest dimensions consistently appeared around Negative Space, Touch Parity, Token Discipline, Accessibility, Audience Fit, and Local Relevance, frequently reaching 9–10 scores with minimal refinement.

Negative Space performed strongest because concepts prioritized silhouette recognition and structural balance over decorative detail. Audience Fit also scored highly due to the intake process, Austin-based research, and scoring framework creating stronger alignment between business goals and visual direction.

The weakest areas remained Data Freshness, Trademark Safety, and Sign-readiness because they depended on external validation rather than design execution alone.

Even with these constraints, all three clients exceeded the 8+ approval threshold, validating the governance model and producing approximately $9,000 in proposal value.

## Reflections: Tools, Concepts, and What Comes Next

### Key tools and concepts

This project combined Claude Desktop Max 20x, Paper Desktop, Obsidian, Firecrawl, Git, and Google AI Studio (Gemini) into a governed design workflow.

Claude Desktop coordinated the 25-agent Council, Paper supported visual production, Obsidian served as the Studio knowledge vault, Firecrawl automated competitive research, Git preserved project history, and Gemini enabled adversarial review.

Key concepts reinforced included multi-agent orchestration, 6-axis approval systems, Continuous Intelligence, adversarial validation, and moving design workflows toward measurable quality engineering.

### Time and challenges

This project took approximately 3 hours.

The largest challenge was calibrating the 6-axis scoring framework to remain objective while managing multi-agent orchestration. Maintaining consistent application of the 3-second test, Golden Ratio, and approval thresholds required several refinement cycles to avoid evaluation drift.

The process shifted my perspective from subjective design execution toward operating as a quality-control engineer, where validation and scoring become part of delivery rather than post-review activities.

Adversarial review through Gemini also exposed subtle biases that would have remained hidden inside a single-model workflow.

### Looking ahead

I completed this project to learn how to build a professional AI-augmented design studio using a 25-agent Council, structured approvals, and Continuous Intelligence workflows.

The experience reinforced how governance, scoring systems, and automation can transform creative work into a repeatable production pipeline while maintaining quality thresholds above 8/10.

The next capability I want to explore is integrating automated project management and client portals that connect project status, budgets, and feedback directly to design outputs while preserving the local-first workflow model.
